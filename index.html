What This Is

NOT FOR
EVERYONE

Most men pick a lane. Get big and slow, or get lean and fast, and quietly let the other side go. Run the Weights does not make you choose. This is a hybrid athlete program built around your race date, your training days, and where you’re actually starting from, not a fixed calendar you have to fit yourself into. You add weight to the bar every week. You also show up to your 5k, your half marathon, or your century ride actually ready, not just surviving it.

The strength side is the same non-negotiable Arnold Split underneath everything here: three days on, one off, bench and squat adding load every single week for three weeks before a deload resets you for Wave 2. Underneath that runs a second engine, a running plan built off your actual race date, current mileage, and experience, that layers onto the same week without wrecking your legs before leg day or your legs before your long run. Hit chest and back in the morning, your easy run or tempo work the same day if the calendar calls for it. Nothing here is guesswork stitched together from two separate apps.

This was built by a former Division 1 cross country captain who also spent years in the weight room, not a lifter guessing at running volume, and not a runner guessing at what a real hypertrophy block looks like. Both sides of this program come from actually having done both, including the mistakes that come from doing them wrong first.

By the time you hit your race, you squat heavier, press heavier, and you can still run the distance you signed up for. Most men never build both at once because they assume they have to choose. You do not.

Strength First
Bench press, barbell squat, back squat, and OHP add load every single week of each wave. Not when you feel like it, every week. Progressive overload on the primary compounds is how your body changes. Everything else is secondary.
Twice a Week
Every split option in this program, 3 through 6 days, hits each muscle group at least twice a week. Fewer days means fewer body regions per session, not less frequency. The second session of the week always attacks the same muscles from different angles than the first, so you never adapt and stagnate. Frequency is what separates serious programs from gym noise.
Move Like an Athlete
Hip 90/90 switches, couch stretch, and goblet squat holds are programmed before every leg day, not as an afterthought, but as direct prep for squat depth and posterior chain loading. Tight hips don't just limit your squat. They shift load to your lower back. Fix the hips; fix the lift.
Built to Last
Face pulls, band pull-aparts, and shoulder CARs appear on every push and pull day. External rotator health is the reason heavy bench pressers blow out their shoulders, and the reason this program's pressing volume is sustainable no matter how long your plan runs.
Two Engines, One Plan
Built by a former Division 1 cross country captain who also lifts seriously, not a runner's guess at a hypertrophy block or a lifter's guess at a training run. The running side scales to your actual race, your actual mileage, your actual timeline, then sits on the same calendar as your lifting so the two never collide.

Your Numbers

BEAT YOUR
LAST SELF

Every lift runs on double progression: two working sets, taken to failure. When your best set hits the top of the rep range on a true failure set, the app prompts the next weight jump — 5 lbs upper body, 10 lbs lower body. Until then the target is simple: beat last session by one rep. Reps dropping back after a weight increase is expected — that is a step up, not a setback. The cards below track every lift you log: this week vs last, the 4-session trend, all-time gain, and how often you actually took your top set to failure.

MovementHoldTime
Hip Flexor Stretch (couch stretch)
2 sets × 60 sec/side
30 sec
~7 min
▼
The single most important mobility exercise for anyone who trains legs. Tight psoas and rectus femoris limit squat depth, increase lower back stress, and flatten the glute response. Rear foot on a bench or couch, front shin vertical, drive your hip toward the floor. Hold the tension — don't wiggle around. 60 seconds is the minimum effective dose for actual tissue change.
Hip 90/90 Switches
2 sets × 10/side
30 sec
~5 min
▼
Sit with both knees bent at 90° — one in front, one to the side. Rotate your pelvis to switch sides, moving through internal and external rotation of the hip joint. Don't use your hands to push your knee down. Let the range come from actual hip rotation. Consistent sessions of this will measurably change your squat depth over time.
Pigeon Pose
2 sets × 60 sec/side
30 sec
~7 min
▼
Targets the piriformis and external hip rotators — the muscles responsible for hip tightness that presents as knee cave during squats. Front shin as close to horizontal as your current mobility allows. Keep both hips square to the floor. Breathe into the tension. 60 seconds minimum per side. It's supposed to be uncomfortable. That discomfort is the work.
T-Spine Rotation (foam roller)
2 sets × 10/side
30 sec
~4 min
▼
Place the foam roller perpendicular to your spine at mid-back level. Support your head, cross your arms, and rotate to each side. Thoracic mobility is critical for overhead pressing mechanics — a stiff t-spine forces the shoulder to compensate. 10 reps each side, letting the weight of your arms rotate you further each rep.
Band Pull-Aparts
2 sets × 20 reps
30 sec
~5 min
▼
Hold the band at shoulder width, arms straight, and pull it apart to chest level — ending in a "T" position. Pinch shoulder blades together hard at end range. This trains the posterior deltoid and external rotators, directly counterbalancing the internal rotation load of pressing. The shoulder maintenance you do on rest day is the reason this shoulder will still train heavy at 40.
Face Pull with External Rotation
2 sets × 20 reps
45 sec
~5 min
▼
Light load — rotator cuff activation, not strength stimulus. Pull to your face with high elbows, then add external rotation at the end: press handles up and outward so your hands end above your elbows. This end-range external rotation is what makes this version more effective than a standard face pull. Think of this as the direct antidote to every pressing session you've done this week.
Jefferson Curl (very light)
2 sets × 8 reps
60 sec
~4 min
▼
Stand on a box or step, hold 10–25 lbs, and slowly curl your spine down vertebra by vertebra — chin to chest first, then thoracic, then lumbar. Controlled spinal flexion under minimal load builds tensile strength in the spinal erectors. Never heavy. Never rushed. The spine is rounding deliberately — that deliberate control is what makes it safe and beneficial.
